Industry Analysis: profitable today, harder each year

The industry is US apps that let amateur runners and cyclists record, share, and compare workouts, as of mid-2026. Defined by need, it includes Garmin, the largest sports-watch maker, whose Connect app is building the same social layer.

Company. Strava is Instagram for workouts: the feed, kudos (likes), and segment leaderboards turn a solo workout into something friends see and strangers rank. The workout is the post. Rivals have matched the software; none has the crowd: 195 million registered athletes and leaderboards that rank everyone who has run a given stretch of road. Added value, what disappears without Strava, is narrower: remove Strava and athletes keep their hardware and coaching but lose the one network spanning every brand, shrinking as Garmin Connect closes in. Strava captures value with a $79.99 subscription for the features the crowd makes valuable, and API limits stop outside apps from rebuilding them. It is buying into coaching instead: Runna, The Breakaway, and AI summaries, the layer Garmin and AI assistants are commoditizing. Effect on attractiveness: positive but narrowing.

Customers. Athletes build the product, 14 billion kudos in 2025, and most never pay for it. Switching costs are social, not data: workouts export, followers do not. A 2023 increase to $79.99 drew a backlash and an apology; since then Strava has raised the effective price via the paywall and a $149.99 Runna bundle. Effect: positive, with a price ceiling.

Suppliers. Athletes are also the main supplier, paid in control, not cash: privacy zones, export rights, no AI training on their data. Beneath them sit two commodity suppliers: map data under the routes and heatmaps, and cloud hosting under the feed and leaderboards. Apple’s 15 to 30% cut of in-app subscriptions is the one real tax, and a 2025 court order lets Strava steer buyers to its website. Effect: negative but small.

Complementors. Device makers matter most and threaten most: Garmin, Apple, Coros, and Wahoo build the watches; Strava builds none. Strava is their complementor, not supplier: it makes the watch more valuable the way apps make the phone more valuable, but no watch needs Strava. It governs apps on its data as Nintendo governed developers: leaderboard data cut from the API, no showing an athlete’s data to anyone, paid access since 2026. Strava cannot cap Garmin, which owns the point of data creation, so each tries to commoditize the other: Strava accepts every device to make the watch interchangeable; Garmin added segments, heatmaps, and, in May 2026, a follower model to Connect to make the social layer interchangeable, making Garmin competitor as well as complementor. Garmin’s leverage has a ceiling: five other makers sync to Strava. Effect: negative and worsening.

Competitors. Garmin Connect gives away segments and heatmaps that Strava charges for; its paid tier launched at $69.99, $10 below Strava. Instagram offers a larger audience without the data that makes a post credible. Strava sits between: too thin on analysis for Garmin, too narrow in audience for Instagram, but the feed where the number on the post is verified. Effect: negative.

Overall attractiveness. Free content and a converting paywall set today’s profit; Garmin moving into Strava’s layer sets the direction. Garmin’s fitness segment earned $2.36 billion in 2025 at a 31% operating margin, so the complementor with the sensors can take more of the surplus. The crowd may be worth more strategically than the subscription monetizes: the social graph and years of segment history could attract a device maker, even though owning Strava would weaken the neutrality that makes it valuable.

Activity Map and Interactions: defended against apps, exposed to Garmin

Exhibit 1 maps six strategic choices and eighteen activities.

InteractionX → YY → XFit & defensibility
A2 and A5: accepting any device, and ranking athletes on segments.A leaderboard is only a competition if everyone who ran the road is on it; accepting every device makes Strava’s ranking complete, so a Garmin-only board carries less weight even among Garmin owners.Segments give owners of any device a reason to sync after each workout, so makers build the sync their customers demand.Reinforcing, and the hardest pair to copy, because Garmin can build segments, and has, but only for Garmin owners.
A11 and A16: charging for depth, and limiting apps to an athlete’s own data.The paywall sets the API’s scope: whatever moves behind the subscription leaves the API, as full leaderboards did in May 2020, cut from 44,000 apps the same day.The closed API turned substitutes into add-ons for people who already pay; VeloViewer, an analytics site, reported that over 70% of its payers already subscribed to Strava.Reinforcing; the paywall decides what the API withholds, and the API protects the paywall. The cost is developer trust.
A10 and A11: keeping the social core free, and charging for depth.Because uploads, the feed, kudos, and clubs cost nothing, everyone joins, which makes the paid leaderboards complete and the heatmaps dense; free users are the raw material subscribers pay for.The subscription funds a free tier without advertising, which keeps the feed clean enough to keep posting to.Reinforcing, and the pair most exposed if Garmin gives paid features away, since Strava cannot make the free core any freer.

Conclusion: implications for defensibility. Rivals copy single activities, not the combination: a network open to every device (A2), complete leaderboards (A5), a free core (A10), and a paywall outside apps cannot rebuild (A11, A16). Garmin and Apple can copy every feature but not the underlying trade-off, device neutrality, since their software sells hardware. That neutrality means Strava cannot punish Garmin without hurting its users; its 2025 patent suit against Garmin lasted 21 days. Well defended against other apps; exposed to the device maker that controls a key point of data creation. My take: the moat is the crowd, not the code. It lasts as long as the crowd stays, and Strava does not own the crowd.
