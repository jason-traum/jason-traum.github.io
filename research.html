The analytics package, from 10 business days to 3

I led the automation of our thousand-slide monthly analytics package. The code took a few weeks. Getting people to trust it took about three months. It’s still running without me.

How I got people to trust it

It pulled more than 4,000 data fields from Bloomberg, FactSet and team workbooks that were all built differently. Senior people worried they’d lose the ability to fix numbers by hand, and they’d seen improvements fail before. So I split it into modules that could run on their own, logged every step, and ran it next to the old process until the numbers matched, sitting with people one at a time. The skeptics ended up shaping the checks. I wrote a one-page runbook so anyone could run it.

I also moved the whole chartbook into the new brand template and redrew a lot of its charts so they were easier to read.

The chart palette

Marketing and operations had argued about our chart colors for 18 months. I finally just stepped in with a palette I’d tested on our real charts, everyone agreed, and it became the Wealth Management standard. I became the unofficial color czar.

How it got made

Advisors said they couldn’t tell the lines apart on printouts and office screens. I kept asking one question, whether an advisor could read the chart printed out in a client meeting. The approved brand colors were all dark, cool shades with very little contrast between them. They worked for marketing, but charts were most of what my team owned. So I derived lighter versions of some of the approved colors, hand-picked a few new ones, and tweaked them until they worked for our charts. I tested every option on our real charts with fake numbers and brought side-by-side examples and real color analysis to firmwide marketing instead of an opinion. Firmwide and Wealth Management marketing both signed off, and I never heard a complaint about it from an advisor.

I also wrote a VBA script that gave our Excel heatmaps smoother color transitions that weren’t so grey in the middle. My team adopted it for several deliverables, including the stock and bond indicator our CIO sends out every week, which I helped develop.

The Magnificent 7 gap, and a newsfeed for advisors

  • On the OCIO team, my style scores showed the funds in our models, active and passive, were all slightly underweight the Magnificent 7, and that drove our underperformance against the S&P 500.
  • We’d publish research on a Tuesday, and by Friday advisors were emailing to ask for it. The research was fine; it just got lost in their inboxes. So I pitched our COO a newsfeed-style format, with short posts timed to when advisors actually needed them. In April 2026 I built a working prototype.

My job at Morgan Stanley

Most of my job at Morgan Stanley was the research behind what about 25,000 financial advisors told their clients.

I worked on the quant frameworks behind our model portfolios and drafted the allocations across risk profiles. I was one of two people writing the daily positioning note, and some days I wrote all of it. In most issues of our monthly Topics in Portfolio Construction I was fully responsible for several pages. Before that, on the OCIO team, I ran the trade process after our investment committee approved a trade, across 30+ model portfolios with $70B in them.

Portfolio construction

Under a lot of it was portfolio construction: deciding how much of each investment a portfolio should hold, and balancing the return we wanted against the risk we could live with.

  • At Guggenheim I optimized multi-asset fixed income model portfolios and ran Monte Carlo simulations to weigh risk, return and liquidity for our portfolio managers. I also automated our monthly analytics end to end, which cut errors by over 95%. One manual step went from 2.5 hours to 15 minutes, and a 24-hour run went down to 30 minutes.
  • On the OCIO team at Morgan Stanley I redesigned our trade analytics so sector weights, style tilts and factor exposures updated instantly across 30+ model portfolios with $70B in them.
  • In the Global Investment Office I worked on the quant frameworks and allocations behind our model portfolios, our weekly stock and bond indicator, a tactical fixed income framework and our capital market assumptions, including crypto, plus a separate report on how much crypto to hold.
  • I also did a lot of research on concentrated stock positions, co-developed our Equity Vulnerability Score, and redesigned the HOPES Excel tool advisors use with clients who hold too much of one stock.

How I used AI as my analyst in a strategy sim

My team took a money-losing department store from $93 million to about $3.4 billion, more than 2x any other team’s final market value and the best result across every section of the course.

It was Pivot or Perish, the retail strategy sim in MKTG 6110, a core class nearly every first-year takes. We started with $287 million of debt, and AI use was allowed.

What I gave it

I ran the AI side in Claude. Every round I gave it the sim’s manual, our class slides and lecture notes, and the 16 or so reports the game puts out, and had it keep one model of every lever and every result for all seven companies.

The questions I asked it

  • Dig deep. Think about the models, the marketing, and what’s changed between rounds.It worked out how the game scores you, which the reports never say: each quadrant adds up two ratings, and customer service counts in two of the four. So service was where our money did the most.
  • That’s it? You don’t think we should test out more changes?Its round 3 plan was small and safe. We went bigger: 40 more stores, the best private brand quality and $7 million more on service. That round showed us what drove the rest of the game.
  • Explain why opening new stores still isn’t worth it. Is that just a result of the sim?More stores only moved one rating, about 0.1 for every 10 stores. Catching a rival would have taken about $350 million of debt, so we stopped opening them.
  • We’ve almost saturated our favorite segment. Should we go after our next favorites without cannibalizing it?We kept our positioning and pushed into the two fastest-growing segments.

How we used it

We used the game’s one undo as a free test market: an extreme round to see what every lever did, then undo it and keep what worked. Before every submit we wrote down what would tell us we were wrong. My teammates and I made the calls.

The one time I let it decide, in the last round, I asked it to pick between two options and went with its answer, cutting spend for profit. Market value fell 1.4% because the sim rewarded growth. Now I ask what each option risks and decide myself.

Sources: our decision log and each round’s reports, and the class results our professor shared.
