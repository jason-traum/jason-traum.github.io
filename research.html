My job at Morgan Stanley

I wrote the daily positioning note, built the quant frameworks behind our model portfolios, including a stock-bond indicator several teams used, and worked on the yearly capital market assumptions, which set the expected return and risk for every asset class in the models. The chart palette

My palette became the Wealth Management standard. It was kind of my first time leading people, and I became the unofficial color czar.

How it got made

Marketing and operations had cycled through chart palettes for 18 months, arguing about the brand, while advisors said they couldn’t tell the lines apart on printouts and office screens. I kept asking one question, whether an advisor could read the chart printed out in a client meeting. The approved brand colors were all dark, cool shades with very little contrast between them. They worked for marketing, but charts were most of what my team owned. So I derived lighter versions of some of the approved colors, hand-picked a few new ones, and tweaked them until they worked for our charts. I tested every option on our real charts with fake numbers and brought side-by-side examples and real color analysis to firmwide marketing instead of an opinion. My palette became the Wealth Management standard. It was kind of my first time leading people, and I became the unofficial color czar.

My team’s move to GenAI

I led my team’s move to GenAI and became the guy people came to when they couldn’t get it to do what they needed.

The agents I built, and what I used it for

We used it for research, brainstorming and drafting, and the time it saved went into deeper work, not fewer hours. I also built about eight internal AI agents, mostly for my team. One automated a really manual part of making our chartbook, one came up with chart ideas from news, market moves and advisor feedback, and one was a house-style writing assistant that up to about 20 people used.

The analytics package, from 10 days to 3

I led the automation of our thousand-slide monthly analytics package. The code took a few weeks. Getting people to trust it took about three months.

How I got people to trust it

It pulled more than 4,000 data fields from Bloomberg, FactSet and team workbooks that were all built differently. Senior people worried they’d lose the ability to fix numbers by hand, and they’d seen improvements fail before. So I split it into modules that could run on their own, logged every step, and ran it next to the old process until the numbers matched, sitting with people one at a time. The skeptics ended up shaping the checks. I wrote a one-page runbook so anyone could run it, and it’s still running without me.

Two more

  • On the OCIO team, the people I was supposed to learn from left within weeks, and I became the team’s only quant. I rebuilt their processes. My style scores showed every active manager in our models was underweight the Magnificent 7, and that explained over half of one quarter’s underperformance.
  • Advisors kept emailing us for research we’d already sent them. The research was fine; it just got lost in their inboxes. So I pitched our COO a newsfeed-style format, with short posts timed to when advisors actually needed them.

How I used AI as my analyst in a strategy sim

My team took a money-losing department store from $93 million to about $3.4 billion, more than 2x any other team’s final market value and the best result across every section of the course.

It was Pivot or Perish, the retail strategy sim in MKTG 6110, a core class nearly every first-year takes. We started with $287 million of debt, and AI use was allowed.

What I gave it

I ran the AI side in Claude. Every round I gave it the sim’s manual, our class slides and lecture notes, and the 16 or so reports the game puts out, and had it keep one model of every lever and every result for all seven companies.

The questions I asked it

  • Dig deep. Think about the models, the marketing, and what’s changed between rounds.It worked out how the game scores you, which the reports never say: each quadrant adds up two ratings, and customer service counts in two of the four. So service was where our money did the most.
  • That’s it? You don’t think we should test out more changes?Its round 3 plan was small and safe. We went bigger: 40 more stores, the best private brand quality and $7 million more on service. That round showed us what drove the rest of the game.
  • Explain why opening new stores still isn’t worth it. Is that just a result of the sim?More stores only moved one rating, about 0.1 for every 10 stores. Catching a rival would have taken about $350 million of debt, so we stopped opening them.
  • We’ve almost saturated our favorite segment. Should we go after our next favorites without cannibalizing it?We kept our positioning and pushed into the two fastest-growing segments.

How we used it

We used the game’s one undo as a free test market: an extreme round to see what every lever did, then undo it and keep what worked. Before every submit we wrote down what would tell us we were wrong. My teammates and I made the calls.

The one time I let it decide, in the last round, I asked it to pick between two options and went with its answer, cutting spend for profit. Market value fell 1.4% because the sim rewarded growth. Now I ask what each option risks and decide myself.

Sources: our decision log and each round’s reports, and the class results our professor shared.
