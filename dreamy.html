How I built Dreamy

Chatbot coaches improvise. Dreamy runs published rules on your data, and AI only explains them.

Dreamy is an AI running coach I built with Claude Code over twelve weeks while I was still working full time at Morgan Stanley. It's for people like me who don't have a reliable 9 to 5, have a busy social life, and still want to run their best marathon. It's technical on purpose: it explains the science behind each workout so you learn to coach yourself. Dreamy's plans come from rules taken from the research, it analyzes every run you sync from Strava, and it works around the days you can't run.

15 runners trained on Dreamy through full race cycles, in the app or on plans I made with its engine. 14 of the 15 rated it above the coach or app they used before. What it didn’t prove

Why I built it

I got into running in 2023, when a bunch of my friends signed up for a half marathon and I did too. Once I wanted to see how fast I could get, I looked for a training plan, and nothing worked for me. The plans assumed perfect weeks that never existed. Then I started using ChatGPT and thought, wow, this can be so good. But it took a lot of prompting and a lot of learning on my own to know what was good and what wasn’t. I figured there had to be a better way.

How it started

The models kept giving me bad plans, so I read up on sports science and the top running coaches and kept rewriting my prompt until the answers got good. I ran a PR and shared it with my run club. The prompt only grew, past 11,000 words, and I wanted more than a prompt could do. Nothing checked it against the research, you had to be really good at prompting to get something usable, and it didn’t sync with anything or look at your actual runs.

What it does that a prompt couldn’t

With Dreamy, the plan comes from rules taken from the research, it syncs with Strava and analyzes every run, and the same data always gives you the same plan. The biggest difference is that it works around your schedule. You tell it which days you can't run and it rebuilds the week around them, so you might run seven days one week and four the next. It adapts when you miss a workout or a race moves, and it explains every change and the science behind it, so over time you learn to coach yourself.

Two years of prompting taught me the chatbot can’t be the coach, so in Dreamy the rules make the plan and the AI explains it.

    My half marathon forecast as the app showed it in March 2026: ten models, eight with enough data to vote, with HR VO2max carrying the most weight. By April the engine ran nine live models plus a durability adjustment.

    1. Each model gives an estimate and a confidenceIts weight is a base weight times that confidence.
    2. Models that drift from your races lose weightToo-optimistic ones lose it faster. A model 8 percent faster than your races gets zero weight, while a slow one gets there at 10 percent.
    3. Your own races anchor itIt’s a small meta-analysis of your races, pooled with random effects (Paule-Mandel, with the Hartung-Knapp correction). A race that went wrong counts as censored data, and old races you’ve since beaten count as floors.
    4. The range leans slowIt comes from six named sources of uncertainty, because more things go wrong on race day than right. Models that use the same inputs don’t count as separate votes.

    The weights were still set by hand when the main build ended. The part that would tune them against real race results wasn’t running yet.

    How it sees you

    Dreamy keeps a model of you and updates it every day from what you actually ran. Fitness and fatigue follow Banister’s model, with 42- and 7-day time constants. Under that, seven systems each build and fade at their own speed, and on a bad day it only counts 40 to 70 percent of a workout.

    Where these numbers come from

    Time constants in days, from simulator/constants.ts: build then fade. The ranges come from Granata 2016, Mujika & Padilla and Magnusson 2010. The exact values are starting guesses the app was supposed to tune for each runner, and that tuning never went live.

    How much you get out of a workout depends on how ready you were. The research backs the direction, and I picked the exact cutoffs myself.

    The plan

    Every plan aims for the most fitness for the fatigue. Dreamy rebuilds the week in the same order every time, based on what you actually ran and which days you have, then picks each day’s workout from 133 templates.

    Try it: tap the days you can't run

    A simplified version of Dreamy's scheduling rules, running in your browser.

      Long run Quality session Easy Recovery Changed from an open week
        How this demo differs from the app

        This is a simplified copy of Dreamy's week-skeleton.ts and mileage-enforcement.ts. Weekly totals land within about a mile of the target. It places runs in the same order, keeps the same 48 hours between hard days and uses the same mileage rules, and easy days stay at or under 80 percent of the long run. In the app you set how many runs and quality sessions you want each week; here they follow the weekly miles (4 runs and one quality session at 25 miles or less, up to 7 runs above 50). The real app also looks at your fitness, fatigue, the weather and your recent runs before it picks each workout.

        The six rules it follows
        1. Days you can't run come off first. Everything else gets planned around them.
        2. The long run goes on your preferred day. If that day is gone, it moves to the other weekend day, then to the nearest open day.
        3. The day after the long run is a recovery run.
        4. Quality sessions go on your preferred days, never next to the long run, at least 48 hours apart, and never longer than the long run. If those days are gone, they move to any open day that fits. Once Dreamy has learned how fast you recover, it uses your recovery time instead of 48 hours.
        5. Other open days get easy runs until you reach the number of runs you set for the week, and the rest are rest days. That's how you can get four runs one week and seven the next.
        6. Miles are rebalanced to hit the weekly target. Easy days take the changes first, up to 3 miles each, and no run goes below 2 miles. Quality days and the long run change last. If there's no way to hit the target, it tells you instead of overloading the runs you have.
        What happens after the week is set

        Then the daily engine picks the actual workout for each day from 133 templates. It simulates seven training systems one day at a time and redoes the rest of the week every day based on what you actually ran.

        A few safety rules override everything else. You get a forced rest day after 14 straight days of running, when you say you're sick, or when your readiness drops below 25 out of 100. The day before a race is a shakeout of 4 miles or less, and there's nothing hard in the three days before it.

        Every change shows what moved, and you can undo it.

        More on the research

        I did an absurd amount of research for it. I used AI to find every marathon plan and sports science paper I could, and gave it a ton of direction from my own marathon training. I started with an LLM writing the plans, but that got expensive and unreliable, so I switched to a rules-based engine. Then I ran real runners' histories through it, had LLMs grade the plans against the research and suggest changes, and reviewed every round myself.

        Testing it

        Can ten models beat a free race calculator?

        A free race calculator takes one recent race and predicts your other times. Dreamy’s forecast sees that race plus every run you’ve logged, so it should do better. So I built a lab to check.

        Each forecast gets scored on its error, on CRPS, which grades the whole range, and on how often the true time lands inside the range. A change only goes in if it’s never worse than the calculator at any distance.

        1. A fake runnerTrue fitness known every day for 15 months
        2. Their training logRuns, heart rate and races, the way a watch and Strava record them
        3. The same engine as the appA forecast every month
        4. ScoredAgainst the truth and against the calculator

        33 fake runners of 11 types. Two of the types are built to break the engine’s assumptions. In one, speed falls off with distance on a different curve than it expects. In the other, fatigue piles up faster than its model allows.

        Two kinds of runners are still far off. Without a heart-rate strap, forecasts come out 12 to 16 percent too slow from the 5K to the half. For beginners who never race, they come out 27 percent too fast at the marathon, and there’s no race to check them against.

        What I learned from the beta

        How I asked

        I asked each runner myself, and they’re all running friends.

        • Heart-rate VO2max can flatter you.

          One runner's heart-rate model said VDOT 54 while their races said 46 to 48. Now any estimate that runs way ahead of real races gets set aside.

        • Most of the early bugs were in the data, not the models.

          One runner's import quietly failed on 922 of 1,211 runs.

        • Plans have to know about races.

          After a race, one plan jumped 30 percent in mileage. The week after a race is now capped at a 15 percent increase.

        • Related fixes have to ship together.

          One runner's forecast only came out right once four model fixes landed at the same time, so model changes now go out together.

        • Real histories caught bugs my tests missed.

          A race goal saved as 71 seconds instead of 71 minutes, and training blocks that got duplicated.

        • Three models can outvote real races.

          For one runner, three models that measure the same thing agreed with each other and made the marathon forecast 15 to 20 minutes too fast.

        • The code reviews found real problems.

          Codex caught that the forecast's range was about 7 times too narrow, and that heat was counted twice for marathons.

        • I froze features before my own marathon.

          For the two and a half weeks before it, nothing new shipped, so nobody's predictions moved under them.

        How I set the repo up for working with AI

        I set the repo up for working with AI. A CLAUDE.md sets the rules, like keeping the engine away from the database and never rewriting the VDOT math. When I corrected something, it went into a lessons file as a new rule. A test fails the build if any API route doesn't say who's allowed to call it, and every change runs type checks, lint, all the tests and a production build.

        The big platforms, and why I stopped

        I wrapped up the main build in April, the day after the Jersey City Marathon, and turned off the scheduled jobs so it stopped costing money. I still use the engine to make training plans, for me and for other runners.

        Since Dreamy went live the big platforms kept shipping. Strava, which has more than 200 million users and already owned Runna, launched a plug-in that lets Claude read your Strava data, so anyone with Claude can ask about their own runs without a separate app. Runna added heat-adjusted paces six months after Dreamy had them, and Garmin bought TrainingPeaks.

        What the beta did and didn’t prove

        The beta showed runners stuck with it. It didn’t prove two things. One is that the race forecasts are accurate, which I set at about 50 forecasts matched to real race results. The other is that anyone would pay. Nobody was asked to, and a heavy user would have cost $5 to $20 a month in AI calls against my goal of under $5.
