I bought a print in 2022 that I love. It has hung in three apartments and never looked right. My wall in New York took two years to get right, and almost every mistake was size and spacing, not taste.

Walldrobe fixes that. You photograph one wall and mark the art you already own. It gives you finished walls drawn on your own photo, with real prints in real frame sizes, the price, and where every nail goes.

It's for people in their late twenties to late thirties who just moved, or just moved in with someone. They own a few pieces they've rehung in every apartment, and they don't know what size to buy, how high to hang it or how far apart. Today they rehang the same prints, or try Pinterest, a tape measure and painter's tape, or buy a matching gallery set that ignores what they already own.

It started as a class venture called Walls on Rotation, after 15 neighbors in my New York building messaged me when I offered prints to borrow. The long-term model is Rent the Runway, for art: the frames stay up and the prints swap.

The details

Same wall, your preferences

These are all the same blank wall, from one photo. The only thing that changes is the preferences sheet: lined up or mixed, calm or full, warm or cool, black, white, oak or brass frames, and how many mats. Each one is a real wall the app built, with real prints you can buy.

What it does

You pick one wall or a whole home. A whole home starts with the art and empty frames you already own, then a photo of each wall, and spreads your pieces across all of them. On a phone, the photo button opens the camera.

Then you scroll finished walls drawn on your photo at true scale, best first, each with its price. Tap any piece to swap it for another that fits the same frame, keep it in every wall, or favorite it. If you love the layout but not the art, it puts new art in the same frames. Everything has Undo. Then you pick one frame look for the set, get the list of what to buy, and get the nail spots.

The calls I made

I ran Walldrobe as the product manager and the tech lead. Claude Code wrote the code. These are the decisions that shaped it.

  1. Rules do the geometry. Models only do taste. The layout is plain code: 57 in to center, 2 to 3 in between frames, the bottom 8 in above the couch. Models only read the photo and judge how the art looks. Spacing has right answers and taste doesn't, and I learned on Dreamy that letting an LLM write plans gets expensive and unreliable.
  2. Your photo never leaves your phone. A photo of your apartment going somewhere you didn't expect is one of the things that makes people hesitate, so the wall reader runs in the browser. It also means reading a photo costs me nothing to serve.
  3. Build every wall once, then re-rank. The engine builds every wall that fits and ranks them as a separate step. When you favorite or swap something, the list reorders right away instead of rebuilding. That let me replace settings like "structured, 6 pieces, balanced" with a list you scroll, because people pick walls by looking at them.
  4. The plan has to be true when the box arrives. A frame on the wall is its sold size plus the moulding. Society6 prints come with a white border printed on, so the app draws the paper and the border and never mats them twice. It only suggests a mat when you can actually buy the smaller print that goes inside it. If it says buy an 11 x 14 frame, that frame has to fit.
  5. Color the way painters see it. Every piece's color is measured. The first math called blue and orange analogous, which any painter knows is wrong, so I moved the scoring to the painter's color wheel.
  6. Taste from picking between two, not a form. You choose between two pieces at a time, and the two are about equally good, so a pick tells it what you like, not which one is simply nicer. Next it shows a pair about what it knows least. About 50% is enough for good walls.
  7. Tester feedback beats my plan. One tester set a $125 budget and got no walls. Now a tight budget steps down until something fits: fewer pieces, then a calmer wall, then no mats, then free art. Every piece of feedback is a logged row, 167 so far, and testers use a frozen beta while I keep building.
  8. I cut my own work. I paused a whole version and restarted from an older interface, because the old screens were better and only missing features. I hid prices, then brought them back as one line: "$1,000 (+$200 to frame)". I cut most of the text on screen.
  9. Lead with the best wall. The first walls always add new art around what you own, fullest first, because the question people have is what this would look like finished.
  10. Every call on the record. 266 dated product decisions, each with the reason. Store images are linked, never copied, and the free photos are credited under their licenses.

How it reads a photo

An image model runs right in the browser: SegFormer, trained on a big set of labeled room photos and shrunk to about 29 MB. It downloads once and stays on the phone. The photo never goes to a server.

It finds the wall's four corners, notices a soffit, and guesses the floor line when furniture hides it. Then it finds the TV, furniture, lamps, outlets and the art already hanging, and you drag any box that's off.

It works out the wall's size from something it knows, usually the TV, and checks that against anything else, like a door or a bed. One typed measurement always wins. It also corrects for furniture standing out from the wall, which makes it look bigger in the photo.

My first version read the ceiling as part of the wall and found none of the art on my own living room. That's why the corners come first now.

How it designs a wall

The engine is about 5,400 lines of plain JavaScript with no dependencies. The same wall and the same taste always give the same result. It starts from hanging rules in inches: 57 in to center, 2 to 3 in between frames, about two thirds the width of the couch, 8 in above furniture, and clear of windows, outlets and the ceiling.

It maps all the open wall around the TV, the lamp and the outlets, and grows groups frame by frame: grids, rows, columns, one statement piece, salon walls and free-form, including two groups on one wall judged together. It works around the art you own. You mark each piece Keep, Maybe or Skip, and a wall that drops one you kept ranks lower.

Then it judges the whole wall: the color scheme on the color wheel, how much of each color, whether an accent repeats, balance, a focal piece, and no two pieces that look almost the same. It ranks in steps, so a wall has to work as an arrangement before color and taste count. Mats follow how framers do it: a structured wall gets all or none, and a loose wall can mix.

How it learns taste

A meter shows how well the taste test knows you. It climbs toward 100% and never gets there, and you can start over any time.

Every pick, save, swap and Not for me teaches it, and it describes your taste back in words, like "black and white, minimal, summer and the sea."

I scored every piece offline with open models: CLIP for 30 plain ideas like retro, moody, botanical and film, plus an aesthetic score for how good it looks. The test only shows strong art. Before that, people got pushed toward black and white just because the color choices in their pairs were weaker.

The art

1,872 pieces from seven sources: free photos from Unsplash, Pexels and Pixabay (641), and prints from House of Spoils (519), Society6 (366), Desenio (246) and Juniper Print Shop (100).

Every piece was looked at and tagged with its subject, mood, style, the rooms it suits and a quality score. Colors and composition are measured from the image: the palette, brightness, contrast, warmth, how busy it is and how much empty space it has.

Shop prints keep their real sizes and prices and link to the shop's own page. Walldrobe sells nothing, isn't paid for links and never re-hosts store images. A free photo keeps its shape, so sizes that would cut it off aren't offered. In Browse you can filter everything like a shop, and hearts teach your taste.

Getting it on the wall

Every wall shows its price: the art plus the cheapest frames for each size in your frame look, with mats where they go. I researched frame prices across Walmart, IKEA, Target, Michaels, Americanflat, Upsimples and Framebridge, and print prices for the free photos at about ten photo labs.

The budget covers art, frames and mats together, and it never dead-ends. If nothing fits, it tries fewer pieces, then a calmer wall, then no mats, then free art, and tells you what it changed.

Get it marks the cheapest and best options for each piece. Hang it gives each frame's hanger type and where every nail goes, with a sheet you can print.

How I build it

I build it with Claude Code: 127 commits in 5 days. The repo has a product doc, an engine spec, every screen in every state, a design doc, 266 dated product decisions, and 167 pieces of feedback from me and testers, each with what changed.

There are about 270 unit tests on the engine and app logic, including 300 random walls checked for overlaps and clearances, plus a scripted walk through the real app in a browser with 447 checks at phone and desktop sizes. It runs before every push.

The screens have been through four rounds. Each one starts with screenshots of every screen, a keep, rework or drop list, and a few structures to pick from.

One night a tester found a budget dead end, frame settings that were hard to find, and a frame width control that did nothing on some walls. All three were fixed the same night. The process also caught two whole shops that never showed up on any wall, because ties between equal pieces broke alphabetically. Now a test fails if any shop stops reaching walls.

What's next, and what's still rough

Next: more art, more questions in the taste test, one shopping list for a whole home, a frame color per piece, and one order for everything. After that comes the original idea: the frames stay up and prints swap by mail, and you can rent an original from a local artist with every payment counting toward buying it.

Still rough: the photo reader sometimes merges furniture or misses a corner, though Fix is one tap away. A whole home can list the same piece twice. And mats show up less than you'd expect, because many shop prints come framed or don't come in the smaller size a mat needs.
