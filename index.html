Dreamy

An AI running coach that plans around the days you can't run

Dreamy started when I was training for my second marathon. Work got busier, my back was acting up, and every plan I tried fell apart once life got in the way. So I turned to ChatGPT. The models kept giving me bad plans, so I read up on sports science and the top running coaches and kept rewriting my prompt until the answers got good. I ran a PR and shared it with my run club. As the models got better I needed less of the prompt, but I wanted more than a prompt could do. Nothing checked it against the research, you had to be really good at prompting to get something usable, and it didn’t sync with anything or look at your actual runs.

With Dreamy, the plan comes from rules taken from the research, it syncs with Strava and analyzes every run, and the same data always gives you the same plan. The biggest difference is that it works around your schedule. You tell it which days you can't run and it rebuilds the week around them, so you might run seven days one week and four the next. It adapts when you miss a workout or a race moves, and it explains every change and the science behind it, so over time you learn to coach yourself.

I did an absurd amount of research for it. I used AI to find every marathon plan and sports science paper I could, and gave it a ton of direction from my own marathon training. I started with an LLM writing the plans, but that got expensive and unreliable, so I switched to a rules-based engine. Then I ran real runners' histories through it, had LLMs grade the plans against the research and suggest changes, and reviewed every round myself.

Claude Code wrote all of the code. My part was the product, the research and the model. I built it from January to April while working full time, and I still use it to make training plans.

15 runners trained on Dreamy through full race cycles, in the app or on plans I made with its engine. 14 of the 15 rated it above the coach or app they used before.

Six years in asset management

Morgan Stanley and Guggenheim, after a summer at Goldman Sachs

The job

Most of my job at Morgan Stanley was the research behind what about 25,000 financial advisors told their clients. I wrote the daily positioning note, built the quant frameworks behind our model portfolios, including a stock-bond indicator several teams used, and worked on the yearly capital market assumptions, which set the expected return and risk for every asset class in the models. Before that, on the OCIO team, I ran the analytics behind 30+ model portfolios with $70B in them. Chartbooks were a big part of every week, and everything we published went through compliance first.

What I did beyond the job

The parts I’m proudest of weren’t in my job description.

  • I led the automation of our monthly analytics package and got it from 10 days to 3. It was a thousand slides and more than 4,000 data fields from Bloomberg, FactSet and team workbooks that were all built differently. Senior people worried they’d lose the ability to fix numbers by hand, and they’d seen improvements fail before. So I split it into modules that could run on their own, logged every step, and ran it next to the old process for months, writing down every discrepancy. The skeptics ended up shaping the validation steps. I wrote a one-page runbook so anyone could run it, and it’s still running without me.
  • I led my team’s move to GenAI for research, brainstorming and drafting. People came to me to learn how to get it to do what they wanted, and the time it saved went into deeper work, not fewer hours. I also built about eight internal AI agents, mostly for my team. One automated a really manual part of making our chartbook, one came up with chart ideas from news, market moves and advisor feedback, and one was a house-style writing assistant that up to about 20 people used.
  • On the OCIO team, the people I was supposed to learn from left within weeks, and I became the team’s only quant. I rebuilt their processes. My style scores showed every active manager in our models was underweight the Magnificent 7, and that explained over half of one quarter’s underperformance.
  • Advisors kept emailing us for research we’d already sent them. The research was fine; it just got lost in their inboxes. So I pitched our COO a newsfeed-style format, with short posts timed to when advisors actually needed them.
  • Marketing and operations had cycled through chart palettes for 18 months, arguing about the brand, while advisors said they couldn’t tell the lines apart on printouts and office screens. I kept asking one question, whether an advisor could read the chart printed out in a client meeting. I tested every option on our real charts with fake numbers and presented it to firmwide marketing, and my palette became the Wealth Management standard. It was kind of my first time leading people, and I became the unofficial color czar.
