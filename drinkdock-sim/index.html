      Reliability inputs

      The Pennsylvania precedent

      The PLCB ran its own wine kiosks in 2010 and 2011, built by Simple Brands of Conshohocken. Buyers inserted a license, a state employee matched their face over video, and a breath sensor blocked anyone at .02 or higher.

      Only 32 of 100 planned kiosks were installed. The state auditor's review logged about 1,000 malfunctions, Walmart backed out of 23 placements citing mechanical concerns, and the board shut the program in September 2011 after a dispute over more than $1 million in costs.

      Lesson for DrinkDock: the last company to try this in Pennsylvania failed on reliability and on a contract where the vendor carried the downside.

      What "never breaks" has to mean

      • Fewer moving parts than the 2010 kiosk: a locked cooler door that opens after ID and payment beats a robotic dispenser.
      • A fallback path: if the ID or face match fails, send the guest to the bartender instead of dead-ending them.
      • Remote monitoring with alerts before the rush, not after, plus a swap-in unit within hours for any bar in the city.
      • Size for the rush: at stadiums one kiosk handles about 100 sales an hour, so an overloaded kiosk loses more guests than it saves (try it on One night).
      • Uptime written into the contract: bars will ask what happens on the Saturday it jams.
      • Never sell to a minor. Boxxtech advertises catching 95% of fake IDs, so about 1 in 20 fakes gets through, and the liquor license at risk is the bar's, not DrinkDock's.

      Guest behavior

      How the model works

      Every venue is a line. Guests arrive at random, each bartender serves one order at a time, and a guest walks away if the wait outlasts their patience. Patience is spread out, so almost nobody leaves in the first minute and most will give it a few. The math is an M/M/n+G queue with Erlang-3 patience; on test cases it matches a brute-force simulation within half a percent.

      Each venue is solved for its rush, shoulder and quiet hours, once without a kiosk and once with one, then scaled to a year. The kiosk takes can-only orders. Can buyers use it much more when there's a line, and a few cocktail or draft guests switch to a can when the wait is long, which costs the venue margin.

      A venue signs if its yearly gain after rent, software, fees, restocking and power clears the hurdle. Market size scales the simulated venues to real counts for the market you pick.
